The moment someone opens your app for the first time, they are deciding whether to keep it. Good onboarding gets users to the value quickly; bad onboarding buries it behind signups and tours.

## What good onboarding does

- Shows the value in seconds, before asking for anything
- Delays the signup until the user has seen why they should bother
- Guides one clear action, not a tour of every feature
- Lets users skip everything and start using the app
- Feels fast, with no loading screens disguised as tutorials

## The data overload problem

Analytics tools can report dozens of numbers, and most of them are noise. The skill is knowing which few numbers matter for your product.

## The metrics worth watching

- Retention, especially how many users come back after a week and a month
- Activation, the rate at which users reach the app's core value
- Daily and weekly active users, which show your real engaged audience
- Time in app and session depth, which show how sticky the product is
- The conversion that matters to your business, whether booking, buying or subscribing

Downloads, screen views and social mentions feel good and prove little. A metric only matters if acting on it improves the business. If you cannot change a number with your decisions, stop watching it.

## Consistency at scale

As products grow, screens multiply and small style differences creep in. A design system is the shared set of components and rules that keeps everything consistent, from one-page websites to apps with hundreds of screens.

## What a design system contains

- Colour, typography and spacing standards, so every screen uses the same language
- Reusable components, like buttons, forms, cards and navigation, built once and used everywhere
- Naming and documentation, so anyone can find and use the right piece
- Guidelines for how components behave, including error and loading states

A design system makes development faster, because nobody rebuilds a button from scratch. It keeps quality consistent, because the rules are agreed and written down. And it makes onboarding easier, because the "house style" is explicit.

## The risk of over-engineering

A design system for a two-screen product is wasted effort. Build the system as the product grows, adding components when they are reused rather than speculatively.
