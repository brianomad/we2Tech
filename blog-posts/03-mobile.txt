## The big architecture choice

The way you build your app affects its cost, performance and how it feels for years. The three main options each trade one set of benefits for another.

## The options explained

- Native apps are built separately for iOS and Android, using each platform's own technology, offering the best performance and access to device features
- Cross-platform apps like React Native and Flutter share one codebase for both platforms, cutting cost while delivering close to native performance
- Hybrid apps wrap a web experience inside a native shell, cheapest of all but often feel less responsive and smooth

## How to choose

- Native matters most when performance and deep device features are critical, like games or heavy media apps
- Cross-platform is the sweet spot for most business apps, social apps and eCommerce, where speed to market and cost matter
- Hybrid works for simple content apps, but the quality gap is usually not worth the saving

## The maintenance angle

Cross-platform means one team maintains one codebase, which keeps ongoing costs lower than maintaining two native apps. That advantage compounds over the life of the product.

## Match the choice to your product

There is no universally right answer. The we2Tech team builds across these approaches and can recommend the one that fits your budget and goals.

## Two paths to the phone screen

A native app is installed from the app store. A progressive web app (PWA) is a website that behaves like an app: installable, fast and capable of working offline. Each has strengths for different businesses.

## What each gives you

- Native apps offer the best performance, full device access, store presence and reliable push notifications, but cost more and require store review
- PWAs are cheaper to build, reach users through a link with no download, update instantly and are increasingly capable with offline support and notifications

## How to decide

- For booking, content, catalogues and tools, a PWA or web app often delivers everything you need at a fraction of the cost

- Consumer products that rely on app-store discovery favour native; anything you can drive traffic to works well as a PWA

## The honest middle ground

Many businesses launch a web app or PWA first, validate demand, then build native when the product earns it. This is often the smartest use of budget.

## The download trap

A big download number feels great and proves little. Thousands of installs that are uninstalled in a week is not success, it is a signal that something is wrong.

## The metrics that matter

Install analytics before launch, not after. You cannot measure what happened before you started measuring, and early decisions are the ones that matter most.

## The double-edged tool

Push notifications are one of the most powerful retention tools an app has, and one of the fastest ways to lose users. The difference is discipline.

## What works

- Notify about things the user actually cares about: their order, booking or activity
- Ask permission at the right moment, when the app's value is already clear
- Let users choose what they receive, like promotions versus reminders
- Keep messages short, relevant and clearly valuable
- Respect local time so notifications arrive at a sensible hour

## What loses users

- Sending a promotion to someone who downloaded the app yesterday for a different purpose
- Notifications that are really advertisements with no value to the receiver
- Asking for permission on the first open, before the user has seen any reason to say yes
- Sending more than a message or two a week without clear benefit

## Test and measure

Watch opt-out rates by notification type. A rising opt-out rate is your clearest feedback that a campaign is wrong. If users are disabling notifications, no frequency tweak will save the strategy.

## Treat it as a privilege

Every push is an interruption. If it does not help the user, it hurts the app. The we2Tech team can help you design a notification strategy that users actually appreciate.

Every app that collects user data is a target. Security is not a feature you add at the end; it is a set of decisions made throughout the build.

## The checklist

- Encrypt data in transit and at rest, so nothing travels or sits unreadable
- Store passwords securely and use proper authentication, never plain text
- Use tokens for sessions so users do not re-login dangerously or needlessly
- Apply the principle of least privilege, so each part can only access what it needs
- Validate all input from users and external systems

- Add a way to detect and respond to breaches, not just a hope they never happen

## The assumption that fails

## What offline-first means

Offline-first design assumes the connection may fail and builds around it: content loads from the device first, data is saved locally, and everything syncs when connectivity returns.

- Let users act offline, like creating a draft or taking an order, and sync later

- Design sync to be automatic and conflict-free when reconnection happens
- Keep the app usable while a slow network is loading, not frozen
