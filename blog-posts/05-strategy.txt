## The two ways to pay

Software development is usually billed one of two ways: fixed price for a defined scope, or hourly (or a monthly retainer) for time spent. Each suits a different kind of project, and both have traps.

## Fixed price, the good and the bad

A fixed price gives you a predictable budget, which suits well-defined projects. The risk is that changes to scope become expensive arguments, so the quality of the original specification decides whether this model works.

## Hourly, the good and the bad

Hourly billing is flexible and honest for evolving projects, letting you redirect effort as you learn. The risk is budget drift if there is no transparency about what is being spent and why.

## What a good fixed quote contains

- A clear list of what is included and what is explicitly excluded
- Milestones tied to payments, so you never pay everything up front
- A defined change process, with how new requests are priced
- What "done" means, including testing and launch responsibilities

## How to choose

If the scope is clear and unlikely to change, fixed price with milestones is often comfortable. If the product will evolve as you learn, a retainer or time-based model is more honest. The we2Tech team will recommend the billing model that matches your project's reality.

category: Strategy

===BODY===
## Scope is the contract

Scope is the agreed list of what a project will and will not do. It determines the price, the schedule and, most of all, whether the project finishes. Poor scope is the root of most software failures.

## How to scope well

- Start from the outcome, then list the minimum features that achieve it
- Write every feature as a behaviour: "the user can X and then Y happens"
- Mark clearly what is out of scope for the first version
- Rank features by must-have, should-have and nice-to-have
- Agree on what "done" means for each feature, before building starts

## The enemies of scope

- Ideas arriving mid-build that feel too small to question
- Stakeholders who want to see "everything" in the first version
- Vague phrases that mean different things to different people
- Features approved because they sound impressive, not because they matter

## How scope changes are handled

Scope will change; that is normal. What matters is that changes are visible and priced, so the project stays honest. A clear change process protects the timeline and the budget.

## The discipline pays

A well-scoped project finishes faster and costs less than an "open" one. The we2Tech team helps you define scope tightly before we build anything.

## From idea to spec

A software requirements document, or spec, is the written agreement on what will be built. It exists so developers, designers and stakeholders all mean the same thing when they say "the project".

## The essential sections

- The goal and success criteria, so everyone knows why the project exists
- The users and their key scenarios, told as concrete stories
- Functional requirements: what the system does, written as behaviours
- Non-functional requirements: performance, security, reliability expectations
- The flow of the core journey, from entry to completion
- Integrations with other systems, payments and data sources
- What is explicitly out of scope, so boundaries are clear

## How detailed is detailed enough

Enough that any competent developer can build without guessing, and enough that two people read the same sentence the same way. Over-documenting everything is waste; under-documenting the core is risk.

## The document is a living thing

A spec is not a stone tablet. It changes as decisions are made, through an agreed change process. The version that ships will not be the version that started.

## Two ways to run a build

Development processes mostly come down to two approaches. Waterfall plans the whole project upfront and builds it in sequence; Agile builds in short cycles, delivering working slices and adjusting as it goes.

## Waterfall, the traditional way

Waterfall suits projects where the requirements are clear, fixed and unlikely to change, and where everything can be planned up front. It gives a predictable budget and schedule, at the cost of flexibility.

Agile delivers value in small working increments, showing you real progress and adjusting to feedback. It handles uncertainty well but requires ongoing involvement and a tolerant approach to the final scope.

- If the product will evolve as you learn, Agile is the honest choice
- If certainty matters more than flexibility, favour more upfront planning

Most good teams blend the two: upfront planning to set direction, then iterative delivery. What you really want is a process that matches your project's uncertainty.

## The slow leak in every budget

Few projects die from one big change; most are killed by many small ones that never get discussed. Scope creep is the accumulation of "small" requests that turn into weeks of work.

## Why it happens

- Ideas feel too small to raise, so they get silently absorbed
- Stakeholders assume changes are free because the project is already running
- There is no shared process for what happens when scope changes

- Agree in writing at the start how changes are handled
- Keep a running list of requests, so nothing is absorbed invisibly
- Price changes against the plan: each request gets a time and cost estimate
- Compare every request against the original goal: does it serve it?
- Keep a contingency budget, and spend it deliberately

## The mindset shift

Saying no to scope is not being inflexible, it is protecting the delivery you promised. Requests are not enemies; silent absorption is.
